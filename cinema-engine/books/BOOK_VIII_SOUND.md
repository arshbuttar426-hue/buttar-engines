# CINEMA SKILL — BOOK VIII: SOUND

> This document extends Books I through VII.
> Everything written here obeys the Constitution.
> No principle in this document may violate any previous book.
> This book becomes the permanent Sound authority of Cinema.
> Sound executes intention. Sound never invents intention.

---

## 1. PURPOSE

Sound does not decorate a scene.

Sound reveals information.
Sound hides information.
Sound directs attention.
Sound establishes atmosphere.
Sound supports the Director's intention.

Sound never creates story.
Sound never changes emotion.
Sound only expresses what previous departments have already decided.

The Director decides what the audience should feel. The Cinematic Language department decides what visual behaviour expresses that feeling. The Camera department decides what is visible. The Lighting department decides how clearly it is visible. Sound decides what the audience hears, when they hear it, and what it means.

---

## 2. FUNDAMENTAL PHILOSOPHY

Sound is information.
Silence is withheld information.

Every sound source must justify its existence.
Every silence must justify its existence.

Nothing is audible without narrative purpose.
Nothing remains silent without narrative purpose.

A fully mixed scene is not "good sound." A fully mixed scene is a statement: everything in this scene matters and nothing is hidden. That statement must serve the Director's intent. If the Director's intent requires the audience to suspect, to doubt, to search — then full audibility betrays the intent.

Sound is the permission the audience receives to hear. Every adjustment to that permission is a narrative act.

---

## 3. RESPONSIBILITY

Sound owns only:

- **Ambient sound** — the baseline sound of the environment (wind, traffic, rain, distant conversation, machinery hum)
- **Foley** — character-generated sounds (footsteps, breathing, cloth rustle, paw pads on pavement, tail thumps)
- **Dialogue audio** — the acoustic characteristics of spoken dialogue (distance, reverberation, clarity, presence)
- **Sound effects** — event sounds (door slams, impacts, explosions, mechanical sounds)
- **Music** — score and diegetic music (music that exists within the scene world)
- **Silence** — the deliberate absence of sound
- **Sound hierarchy** — which sounds dominate, which support, which are subordinate
- **Sound continuity** — the persistence of sound states across scenes
- **Spatial audio** — how sound locates sources in the stereo or surround field
- **Temporal audio** — how sound changes over time within a scene

Nothing else.

---

## 4. WHAT SOUND NEVER OWNS

- Story or narrative purpose (Director)
- Visual grammar or symbolism (Cinematic Language)
- Camera position or movement (Camera)
- Character behaviour or dialogue content (Writer)
- Environment design or props (Production Designer)
- Character positions in space (Spatial Staging)
- Lighting (Book VI)
- Editing rhythm (Editor)
- Render prompts or renderer settings (Prompt Compiler)

Sound reads the documents from previous departments and translates their intent into audio. It does not originate intent.

## 5. SOUND CATEGORIES

### 5.1 DEFINITIONS

| Category | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Ambient | The continuous background sound of the environment | Traffic, wind, rain, distant birds, air conditioning hum, city drone |
| Foley | Character-generated sounds from movement and interaction | Footsteps, breathing, cloth rustle, paw pads, tail wag thumps, ear flutters |
| Dialogue | Spoken words and their acoustic characteristics | Direct speech, muttered asides, shouted warnings, whispered secrets |
| Sound Effects | Discrete event sounds caused by actions or objects | Door slams, impacts, mechanical noises, animal sounds, weather events |
| Music | Score (non-diegetic) or diegetic music within the scene | Orchestral score, radio music, character humming, street musician |
| Silence | The deliberate absence of all sound | Total quiet, near-total quiet with only faint ambient trace |

### 5.2 NARRATIVE JUSTIFICATION

Each sound source must have narrative justification. A sound exists because:
- The environment logically produces it (a street has traffic).
- The story requires it (a character needs to be heard speaking).
- The emotional intent demands it (the Cinematic Language calls for tension or comfort).
- The atmosphere requires it (rain needs rainfall sound to be present).
- The action produces it (a footstep makes a footstep sound).

A sound source that exists only because "it sounds good" has no justification. Remove it.

### 5.3 SOURCE HIERARCHY

Within a scene, sounds are organized hierarchically:

- **Primary sound** — the dominant sound the audience perceives first. Usually dialogue or the most prominent environmental element.
- **Secondary sound** — supporting sounds that enrich the primary. Footsteps under dialogue, ambient under effects.
- **Tertiary sound** — background texture that provides depth without demanding attention. Distant traffic, air movement.
- **Accent sound** — brief, targeted sounds that highlight specific moments. A door creak, a single raindrop, a breath catch.

The hierarchy is determined by narrative priority, not by technical convention.

## 6. SOUND GRAMMAR

### 6.1 PRINCIPLE

Sound Grammar is a dictionary. Each entry maps an abstract concept to its auditory expression.

These are not mixer settings. These are not decibel values. These are descriptions of how sound communicates each idea through hearing.

### 6.2 THE DICTIONARY

#### ISOLATION

The soundscape is sparse. Few sources. Long silences between events. The audience hears the subject's separateness before any dialogue confirms it. Ambient sound is minimal or absent. The subject's own sounds (breathing, movement) become prominent because there is nothing else to hear.

#### AUTHORITY

The subject's voice or presence dominates the soundscape. Other sounds are subordinate or absent when the subject speaks. The acoustic space feels structured — the subject's sound occupies the center of the stereo field, is clear and close. When the subject is silent, the world seems to wait.

#### FEAR

The soundscape is compressed. Close, intimate sounds dominate — breathing, heartbeat, cloth rustle. Ambient sounds feel too close, too large. Unexpected sounds interrupt. The audience should feel the desire to cover their ears combined with the inability to do so. Silence is threatening because it suggests something is being withheld.

#### HOPE

The soundscape opens. Ambient sounds brighten — birds, distant cheerful activity, wind that feels fresh rather than cold. Music, if present, lifts. The subject's sounds become lighter, quicker. The audience should feel possibility in the auditory space.

#### WONDER

The soundscape reveals scale. Vast ambient sounds — wind across a canyon, ocean against cliffs, city from a height. The subject's sounds are small within the larger acoustic space. The audience should feel their own auditory smallness.

#### LONELINESS

The environment may be full of sound — crowded, even — but the subject does not connect with it. Sounds pass by without interaction. The subject's own sounds are the only ones that feel present. The acoustic space around the subject is emotionally empty even if physically noisy.

#### OBSESSION

The soundscape collapses to one source. Everything else is filtered out. The audience hears only what the obsessed character hears. Repetition reinforces obsession — the same sound, the same rhythm, the same word. The world narrows audibly.

#### MEMORY

The soundscape shifts. Familiar sounds feel different — softer, slightly displaced, as if recalled rather than heard. Details are selective — some things are vivid, others dissolve. The audience should feel they are inside a recollection. Present-tense intrusion breaks memory.

#### CURIOSITY

The subject's attention moves audibly — searching, scanning, discovering. The soundscape reveals new details progressively. The audience discovers alongside the subject. Hidden sounds become partially audible. The audience should feel the pull of the unknown.

#### SUSPICION

The soundscape sounds normal but feels wrong. Something is out of place — a sound that should not be there, or the absence of a sound that should be. The audience hears something the subject has not yet noticed. Familiar sounds seem unfamiliar. The audience should feel unease without knowing why.

#### CHAOS

The sound hierarchy dissolves. Multiple sources compete for attention. Sounds are unpredictable, overlapping, clashing. The audience cannot focus. The soundscape mirrors the loss of control.

#### ORDER

The sound hierarchy is strict. Everything has its place. Sounds follow established rhythms. The environment is clean, arranged, intentional. The audience should feel stability and constraint.

#### CALM

The soundscape is gentle. Ambient sounds are soft and continuous. No sharp events. No sudden changes. The subject's sounds are unhurried. The audience should feel the absence of threat.

#### GRIEF

The subject is still. The environment continues sounding — but slowly, as if through resistance. The soundscape feels heavy. The subject's connection to the world is audibly severed. The audience should feel the weight of absence.

#### JOY

Movement is expansive audibly. The subject's sounds occupy more acoustic space than necessary. The environment seems to respond — cheerful ambient sounds, light music, birds. The audience should feel energy.

#### SILENCE

Not the absence of sound, but the presence of auditory waiting. The soundscape holds. Nothing changes. The audience waits. The duration of the silence creates meaning. Silence in cinema is not emptiness; it is the audience hearing their own expectation.

## 7. DIALOGUE AUDIO

### 7.1 PRINCIPLE

Dialogue is not just words. Dialogue is sound. How it sounds communicates as much as what it says.

### 7.2 ACOUSTIC CHARACTERISTICS

| Characteristic | Meaning | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Close/Direct | Intimacy, confrontation, whisper, secret | Close-up shots, private moments |
| Distant/Reverberant | Separation, alienation, public space, memory | Wide shots, flashbacks, characters far apart |
| Muffled/Obscured | Threat, danger, concealment, the unsaid | Characters behind barriers, whispered threats |
| Clear/Crisp | Honesty, directness, authority, truth | Center frame, direct address |
| Breathless/Urgent | Fear, running, excitement, panic | Action sequences, chase scenes |
| Soft/Gentle | Comfort, intimacy, tenderness, sleepiness | Quiet moments, bedtime, reconciliation |
| Loud/Booming | Authority, anger, announcement, command | Leaders, parents, public addresses |
| Echoing | Memory, dream, vast space, isolation | Flashbacks, caverns, empty halls |

### 7.3 DIALOGUE RULES

- Dialogue clarity must match the Director's information control. What the audience "must never miss" must be clearly audible. What they "should miss" may be muffled, distant, or partially obscured.
- Overlapping dialogue (characters speaking simultaneously) creates chaos, urgency, or realism. It must be motivated by the scene's emotional intent.
- Silence after dialogue is as important as the dialogue itself. The duration of the silence determines its weight.
- Dialogue audio characteristics should change within a scene if the emotional arc requires it. A whisper becoming a shout is intensification.

## 8. MUSIC

### 8.1 PRINCIPLE

Music is the most emotionally direct sound. It must be used with precision.

### 8.2 MUSIC CATEGORIES

| Category | Definition | Narrative Use |
|---|---|---|
| Score (non-diegetic) | Music the characters cannot hear | Emotional amplification, genre signaling, rhythm support |
| Diegetic music | Music that exists within the scene world | Character expression, cultural context, time period |
| Character humming/singing | Music produced by a character | Intimacy, habit, emotional state, cultural background |
| Ambient music | Music that blends with the environment | Atmosphere, location identity, subtle mood |

### 8.3 MUSIC RULES

- Music must serve the Director's emotional arc. It should not play continuously — it should enter when emotion needs amplification and exit when silence or ambient sound is more powerful.
- Diegetic music is always spatially located. It has a source in the scene (a radio, a street musician, a character humming). Non-diegetic score is not spatially located — it exists only for the audience.
- Music can contradict the visual for dramatic irony (happy music over a sad scene, tense music over a calm scene). This contradiction must be intentional and motivated.
- Silence after music is more powerful than silence before music. The absence of music after it has been present creates loss.

---

## 9. SILENCE

### 9.1 PRINCIPLE

Silence is an active sound design decision.

Silence is not the absence of sound. Silence is the presence of auditory withholding.

### 9.2 TYPES OF SILENCE

| Type | Definition | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Total silence | No sound at all | Maximum tension, death, transcendence, the infinite |
| Near-silence | Only the faintest ambient trace | Memory, dream, aftermath, waiting |
| Selective silence | Some sounds removed while others remain | Focus — removing ambient to highlight breathing, or removing dialogue to highlight ambient |
| Interrupted silence | A long silence broken by one sharp sound | Shock, revelation, the breaking of peace |

### 9.3 SILENCE RULES

- Silence must be earned. It cannot follow noise without transition. The audience needs to notice the silence.
- The duration of silence determines its weight. One second of silence after dialogue is a pause. Three seconds is significant. Five seconds is monumental.
- Silence is most powerful when it follows a rich soundscape. The contrast creates the meaning.
- Silence after music is grief. Silence after chaos is relief. Silence after dialogue is weight.

## 10. SPATIAL AUDIO

### 10.1 PRINCIPLE

Sound locates sources in space. The audience should hear where things are.

### 10.2 SPATIAL CATEGORIES

| Position | Auditory Expression |
|---|---|
| Left of frame | Sound positioned left in stereo field |
| Right of frame | Sound positioned right in stereo field |
| Center of frame | Sound centered, dominant |
| Behind camera | Distant, reverberant, low presence |
| Off-screen left/right | Sound enters from off-screen edge, creating anticipation |
| Above | Sound from above (rare but effective for authority or threat) |
| Below | Sound from below (underground, hidden, subterranean) |

### 10.3 SPATIAL AUDIO RULES

- The primary sound source (usually dialogue of the primary subject) should be centered.
- Off-screen sounds create curiosity. The audience hears something they cannot see.
- Moving sounds (a car passing, a character walking across frame) should move across the stereo field to match their visual movement.
- Environmental ambient sound should feel enveloping — it comes from all directions, establishing the space.

---

## 11. TEMPORAL AUDIO

### 11.1 PRINCIPLE

Sound changes over time within a scene. These changes are narrative acts.

### 11.2 TEMPORAL PATTERNS

| Pattern | Auditory Behaviour | Narrative Use |
|---|---|---|
| Fade in | Sound gradually becomes audible | Arrival, awakening, revelation, memory emerging |
| Fade out | Sound gradually becomes inaudible | Departure, sleep, death, forgetting |
| Crescendo | Sound increases in volume/intensity | Building tension, approaching threat, growing emotion |
| Decrescendo | Sound decreases in volume/intensity | Release, calm, aftermath, retreat |
| Stutter | Sound starts and stops abruptly | Shock, malfunction, uncertainty, broken rhythm |
| Layering | New sounds added progressively | Complexity increasing, world expanding, information accumulating |
| Stripping | Sounds removed progressively | Simplification, focus, narrowing, isolation |

---

## 12. SOUND CONTINUITY

### 12.1 PRINCIPLE

Sound belongs to the World (Book II). It cannot randomly change.

Sound state is part of the World's persistent state. The World Engine tracks:
- Current ambient sound character in each location
- Current sound effects that are ongoing (machinery, weather, animals)
- Current music that is playing (diegetic)
- Current silence state

### 12.2 CHANGE REQUIRES CAUSE

Sound changes only when causally justified:

| Cause | Effect |
|---|---|
| Character enters new location | Ambient sound changes to match new location |
| Weather changes | Rain sound appears, wind increases, birds stop |
| Door opens/closes | Interior/exterior ambient sound shifts |
| Machine starts/stops | Mechanical sound begins or ends |
| Character action | Footsteps, impacts, object manipulation sounds |
| Time passage | Day sounds transition to night sounds |
| Power loss | Artificial sounds cease, ambient becomes dominant |
| Music source turns on/off | Diegetic music begins or ends |

### 12.3 RULE

Never change sound because it "sounds cinematic." Sound changes because something in the world caused it. If the Director needs a different sound state for emotional purposes, the cause must be written into the scene.

## 13. FOLEY PHILOSOPHY

### 13.1 PRINCIPLE

Foley is the sound of physical existence. Every character movement produces sound. Every interaction with the environment produces sound.

### 13.2 FOLEY CATEGORIES

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Movement | Footsteps, paw pads, crawling, dragging |
| Clothing | Cloth rustle, scarf swish, collar jingle, cap brim shift |
| Breathing | Normal breath, excited panting, fearful gasping, sleeping snore |
| Body sounds | Heartbeat, stomach growl, joint crack, ear flutter |
| Interaction | Paw on pavement, nose sniff, tail thump, head scratch |

### 13.3 FOLEY RULES

- Foley must match the character's physical state. A tired character's footsteps are heavier, slower. An excited character's breathing is faster, shallower.
- Foley must match the environment. Paw pads on dry pavement sound different from wet pavement, from grass, from tile.
- Foley must match the camera distance. Close-up shots have prominent foley. Wide shots have minimal foley — the environment dominates.
- Foley provides the physical reality that makes characters feel present. Without foley, characters are floating voices.

## 15. FREQUENCY AND LEVEL DESIGN

### 15.1 THE FREQUENCY SPECTRUM

Human hearing spans 20Hz to 20kHz. Different frequency ranges trigger different physiological and emotional responses.

| Range | Label | Emotional Effect | Narrative Use | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20-60 Hz | Sub-bass | Physical tension, unease, rumble | Danger, threat, explosion, earthquake | Rumble, subwoofer, low synth |
| 60-200 Hz | Bass | Power, weight, authority | Impacts, explosions, music bass | Kick drum, bass guitar, male voice |
| 200-500 Hz | Low-mids | Warmth, fullness, or muddiness | Body of sound, intimacy vs congestion | Lower vocals, cello, guitar body |
| 500-2000 Hz | Midrange | Presence, clarity, intimacy | Dialogue zone, emotional connection | Human voice, violin, snare |
| 2000-5000 Hz | Upper-mids | Definition, edge, or harshness | Attack, aggression, sibilance | Consonants, cymbals, bite |
| 5000-8000 Hz | Presence | Air, openness, detail | Clarity, realism, spatial awareness | Sibilance, hi-hat, room sound |
| 8000-20000 Hz | Brilliance | Sparkle, air, or harshness | Hope, transcendence, or pain | Cymbals, air, high harmonics |

### 15.2 EMOTIONAL FREQUENCY PROFILES

| Emotion | Key Frequencies | Mixing Approach |
|---|---|---|
| Hope / Uplift | Boost 8-12 kHz | Add harmonic excitement, widen stereo |
| Sadness / Reflection | Emphasize 250-500 Hz | Gentle compression, warm |
| Fear / Tension | Add sub-bass 30-60 Hz | Reduce clarity, add density, close mics |
| Joy / Relief | Widen stereo, boost 5-8 kHz | Full frequency range, open |
| Romance | Boost 10-12 kHz (air), 250-500 Hz (warmth) | Subtle, intimate, close |
| Authority | Emphasize 120-250 Hz | Center voice, close proximity |
| Vulnerability | Reduce 300-600 Hz (thinner voice) | Exposed, minimal processing |
| Nostalgia | Warm low-mids, rolled-off highs | Lo-fi character, tape saturation |

### 15.3 DECIBEL AND LOUDNESS STANDARDS

The decibel (dB) is a logarithmic measure of sound level. In film sound, absolute and relative levels follow established standards:

| Standard | Specified Level | Context |
|---|---|---|
| Reference mixing level | 79 dB SPL or 82 dB SPL | Calibration tone at mix position |
| Program loudness (Netflix) | -27 LKFS ±2 LU | Full program, dialog-gated |
| Program loudness (EBU R128) | -23 LUFS ±1 LU | Broadcast standard |
| Maximum true peak | -2 dBFS (-2.3 dBFS safe) | No samples above this level |
| Loudness range (LRA) | 4-18 LU (ideal), Dialog LRA ≤10 LU | Balance without excessive variation |

**Mixing Level Guidelines:**

| Element | Level (relative) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Dialogue | -12 to -15 dBFS average | Primary element, must be clear |
| Music | -18 to -22 dBFS average | Under dialogue unless scoring |
| Sound Effects | -10 to -20 dBFS (peaks to -8 dBFS) | Dynamic, event-based |
| Ambient | -20 to -30 dBFS | Background texture |
| Overall mix | -10 to -14 dBFS average | Below 0 dBFS peaks |

These are starting points. The Director's emotional intent overrides all numerical guidelines.

### 15.4 EMOTIONAL HIERARCHY OF SOUND

In any scene, the audience's ear seeks a hierarchy. The Sound department must decide which element dominates at each moment:

1. **Dialogue** — audience empathy and connection (default primary)
2. **Score** — theme and emotional momentum (overtakes during emotional peaks)
3. **Ambience** — realism and grounding (supports without demanding attention)
4. **Foley and effects** — tactile energy and physical presence (punctuation)
5. **Silence** — contemplation and release (only when earned)

The hierarchy shifts within a scene. A character's realization may swap dialogue for score + silence. A chase sequence promotes foley and effects above ambience. The hierarchy is dynamic and moment-dependent.

### 15.5 EQ PRINCIPLES FOR CLARITY

Equalization (EQ) shapes the tonal quality of individual sound elements to prevent masking — where one sound obscures another at the same frequency.

**Common EQ interventions:**

| Problem | Solution |
|---|---|
| Muddy dialogue (competing with 200-500 Hz elements) | Cut low-mids on ambient/reverb; high-pass filter non-voice elements at 100-150 Hz |
| Thin dialogue (missing body) | Gentle boost at 200-400 Hz |
| Sibilant dialogue (excessive S/T sounds) | Gentle cut at 5-8 kHz |
| Dialogue masked by music | Sidechain compress music or cut music at dialogue's fundamental frequencies (300-3000 Hz) |
| Boomy ambience (excess low end) | High-pass filter at 60-100 Hz |
| Harshness (excess 2-5 kHz) | Subtle cut, or use dynamic EQ on peaks |

### 15.6 COMPRESSION PRINCIPLES

Compression reduces dynamic range — making quiet sounds louder and loud sounds quieter.

| Use | Attack | Release | Ratio | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dialogue consistency | 10-30 ms | 50-100 ms | 2:1 to 4:1 | Smooth level, no pumping |
| Voice presence | 20-40 ms | 40-80 ms | 3:1 to 5:1 | Voice cuts through mix |
| Sound effect punch | 1-5 ms | 100-200 ms | 4:1 to 8:1 | Impact without distortion |
| Music bus glue | 10-30 ms | 40-80 ms | 1.5:1 to 2:1 | Cohesion without squash |
| Limiting (safety) | 0.1 ms | 50 ms | 10:1+ | Catch peaks, prevent clipping |

## 16. EXECUTION POSITION

Sound works after Lighting (which determines what is visible and how atmosphere behaves) and before Editor (who needs to know sound design to determine editing rhythm).

### 16.1 EXECUTION ORDER

The full execution order is defined in Book I, Section 11 (Execution Order). Sound works after Lighting (which determines atmosphere) and before the Editor (who needs sound design to determine editing rhythm).

Sound reads:
- `camera_specification` (Book III)
- `environment_specification` (Book X)
- `spatial_staging_specification` (Book VII)
- `lighting_specification` (Book VI)
- `cinematic_language_specification` (Book IV)
- `director_report` (Book V)
- `world_state` (Book II)

Sound produces:
- `sound_specification`

### 16.2 WHY SOUND COMES AFTER LIGHTING

Sound must know what the Lighting department has decided about atmosphere. Rain that Lighting has declared present must have rainfall sound. Fog that Lighting has declared must have dampened, muffled acoustics. The atmospheric conditions determined by Lighting directly affect the soundscape.

### 16.3 WHY SOUND COMES BEFORE EDITOR

The Editor determines rhythm, pacing, and transitions. Sound design — especially music, silence, and sound effect timing — directly influences where cuts should occur. A door slam is a natural cut point. A musical crescendo is a natural transition moment. The Editor needs the sound_specification to make informed editing decisions.

## 17. RELATIONSHIP TO OTHER DEPARTMENTS

### 17.1 SOUND AND CINEMATIC LANGUAGE

The Cinematic Language department defines concepts like Fear, Hope, Chaos. Sound implements these concepts audibly. Fear is close breathing and unexpected sounds. Hope is bright ambient and lifting music. Each concept from Book IV has an auditory implementation.

### 17.2 SOUND AND SPATIAL STAGING

Spatial Staging determines where characters are positioned. Sound uses these positions to determine spatial audio — a character at the left of frame should have their voice positioned left in the stereo field. A character at the edge of the group should have their foley (footsteps, breathing) positioned at that edge.

### 17.3 SOUND AND CAMERA

The Camera department determines what is visible. Sound must provide the auditory equivalent — what is audible should match what is visible, unless the Director intends dramatic irony (the audience hears something the camera does not show).

### 18.3 INVARIANT

This specification contains:
- No camera positions.
- No lighting instructions.
- No renderer language.
- No prompt wording.

It contains only: sound sources with narrative justification, dialogue audio characteristics, music descriptions, silence design, sound hierarchy, and continuity. The Camera department determined what is visible. Sound determines what is audible.

This book defines ONLY the Sound department.

- Spatial staging
- Editing systems
- Color grading
