# CINEMA SKILL — BOOK VII: SPATIAL STAGING

> This document extends Books I through VI.
> Everything written here obeys the Constitution.
> No principle in this document may violate any previous book.
> This book becomes the permanent spatial authority of Cinema.
> Every character position in every scene obeys this book.

---

## 1. PURPOSE

Spatial Staging determines where every character stands, sits, or moves in every scene.

The Production Designer builds the physical world.
Spatial Staging decides how characters occupy that world.

These are different responsibilities:
- Production Designer determines what exists (location, objects, atmosphere).
- Spatial Staging determines who is where, facing what, at what elevation, with what relationship to every other character and every environmental element.

A well-designed environment with badly staged characters is a photograph of a theatre set. Spatial Staging makes it drama.

Spatial Staging is the difference between "five dogs in a circle" and "the senior claims the step, the guard watches the street, the tired one curls in the corner, the loud one commands the open ground, and the optimist bounces between them."

---

## 2. FUNDAMENTAL PHILOSOPHY

Every position in space communicates meaning.
There is no neutral placement.

A character who sits on a step is not simply "on a step." They are elevated above others, claiming territory, commanding a wider view, making others look up at them.

A character who faces the street while the group faces inward is not simply "facing the street." They are guarding the perimeter, choosing vigilance over participation, oriented toward threat rather than community.

The Spatial Staging department must answer one question for every character in every scene:

**"Why is this character at this position, at this elevation, facing this direction, at this distance from every other character?"**

If the answer is "because there was space there," the staging is not cinema. It is furniture arrangement.

## 3. SPATIAL GRAMMAR

### 3.1 ELEVATION

Elevation is the vertical position of a character relative to others.

| Position | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Above others | Authority, seniority, dominance, safety (can see threats arriving), separation | A step, a platform, a raised doorway, a wall top, a slope |
| Same level | Equality, engagement, social proximity, confrontation | Same pavement, same floor, same step |
| Below others | Submission, vulnerability, intimacy, deference, childhood | Ground looking up at a step, sitting while others stand, lying down |

Elevation is always relational. A character on a 30cm step is not "high." They are high *relative to* the characters on the ground. The meaning only exists in the comparison.

#### Elevation Rules

- A character claiming authority in a scene must have equal or greater elevation than those they lead. If a leader character cannot physically claim higher ground, the staging must compensate through proximity (centered) or orientation (others face them).
- A character entering submission or deference must lose elevation — sit when others stand, step down when others are on a platform.
- When two characters of equal authority interact, they share elevation. Any height difference between them is a statement of dominance.
- When a character moves from lower to higher elevation during a scene, their authority is increasing. When they move from higher to lower, their authority is decreasing, or they are descending to join.

### 3.2 TERRITORY

Territory is the horizontal position of a character relative to the environment's boundaries and the group.

| Zone | Meaning | Typical Occupant |
|---|---|---|
| Center | Dominance, attention-seeking, exposure, confidence, performance | The loudest, most expressive, or most important character |
| Edge/Perimeter | Protection, observation, readiness to leave, guard duty | Protector characters, cautious characters, those who don't fully trust the group |
| Corner (intersection of 2+ boundaries) | Maximum shelter, minimum exposure, defense, deepest safety | Vulnerable characters, exhausted characters, wounded characters, those seeking rest |
| Threshold/Entry | Transition, liminality, one foot in one foot out, arrival or departure | Leaders arriving or leaving, characters uncertain of their belonging |
| Between others | Mediation, being pulled between, divided attention, alliance | Characters who bridge two factions, or who are torn between two figures |

#### Territory Rules

- A character who belongs to the scene claims their natural territory. A character who does not belong will not settle — they will stand at edges, shift weight, point their body toward exits.
- When territory is contested (two characters want center), one must yield by moving to edge, or they split the space. Both claiming center is a conflict staging.
- A group at rest establishes territories that reveal hierarchy. The highest-ranking member claims the best position (highest, most sheltered, or most commanding). Others arrange around that position.
- When a group moves, the formation leader sets the direction. Others follow in order of rank — the second-in-command closest, the least influential at the rear.

### 3.3 ORIENTATION

Orientation is the direction a character's body and head face.

| Orientation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Body + head toward group | Full engagement, participation, trust (back is exposed to the group) |
| Body toward group, head checking outside | Engagement with divided attention — guard duty while participating |
| Body + head away from group | Disengagement, departure, rejection, refusal, guard duty (fully external) |
| Body away, head turned back | Leadership (physically departing but still directing), or watchfulness conflicting with belonging |
| Side-on to group | Reduced engagement, neutrality, casual observation |
| Directly facing another character | Confrontation, direct address, intimacy, challenge |
| At an angle to another character | Casual conversation, lower stakes, non-confrontational |

#### Orientation Rules

- Body orientation reveals intent. A character whose body faces the exit while talking is planning to leave. A character whose body faces the group while another dog is visible at the edge has chosen the group over the threat.
- Head orientation reveals attention. A character may face one direction with their body to signal social intention while their head points elsewhere to track a target.
- In a group scene, the primary speaker draws all head orientations toward them. When attention shifts, heads shift. A character who does not turn toward the speaker is deliberately ignoring them (too tired, too dominant, too disengaged).
- Ear orientation (for characters with mobile ears) is a finer-grained version of head orientation. Ears track the source of interest even when the head cannot turn. One ear forward, one ear back is the classic "divided attention" posture — participating while monitoring.

### 3.4 PROXIMITY

Proximity is the distance between characters.

| Distance | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Touching | Trust, intimacy, alliance, comfort, protection, dependency |
| Close (within one body length) | Social closeness, friendship, alliance, shared purpose |
| Middle (2-4 body lengths) | Normal conversation, social distance, neutrality |
| Far (5+ body lengths) | Isolation, outsidership, authority, rejection, deep focus |
| Beyond visible | Absence, departure, separation across a cut |

#### Proximity Rules

- Characters who trust each other occupy close or touching distance. Characters who distrust or are in conflict occupy middle or far distance.
- A character who moves closer to another during a scene is building connection. A character who moves away is withdrawing.
- Proximity interacts with elevation. Two characters at the same elevation at close distance is intimacy. Two characters at different elevations at close distance is hierarchy (one looks up, one looks down).
- A character who stands alone at far distance while the group clusters is either the authority (separate by rank) or the outcast (separate by rejection). The staging must make clear which.

## 4. TERRITORIAL MECHANICS

### 4.1 PRINCIPLE

Cinema projects often feature non-human characters (animals, creatures, monsters) whose spatial behavior differs from human social norms. Spatial Staging derives territorial mechanics from each character's species, personality, and role.

### 4.2 CANINE TERRITORIAL BEHAVIOR

For dog characters specifically:

| Behavior | Spatial Expression |
|---|---|
| Pack hierarchy | Highest-ranking dog claims the best position (most elevated, most sheltered, or best vantage). Others arrange in rank order around it. |
| Perimeter guard | The most vigilant dog positions at the edge of the group, facing outward. Head checks the group but body faces threat. |
| Submission | Submissive dogs position lower (lying, head down), avoid direct eye contact, orient away from dominant dogs. |
| Sheltering | Vulnerable or tired dogs take positions against walls, under cover, in corners — back protected, field of view narrowed. |
| Display | Dominant or attention-seeking dogs take open space, stand (not sit/lie), position themselves so they are visible to the whole group. |
| Scent/post marking | Dogs re-claim territory on return. In visual storytelling, this translates to: a character who owns a space physically marks it by sitting in the center, circling before lying, or positioning at its boundary. |
| Following | When a leader moves, the group follows in rank order. The second-in-command is closest. The most subordinate is at the rear. |

### 4.3 CHARACTER-SPATIAL ARCHETYPES

Every character should have a default spatial behavior derived from their personality. This default activates when the character does not have a scene-specific spatial motivation.

| Archetype | Default Spatial Behavior | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Leader | Edge or elevated. Faces group but monitors perimeter. Body oriented toward the group's direction of travel. | Bandu on the step, Scraps at the pavement edge |
| Protector/Guard | Farthest edge. Body oriented outward. Head turned back to group. Closest to exits or threats. | Scraps facing the street |
| Loud/Expressive | Center. Open space. Stands when others sit. Gestures broadly. Occupies maximum visual space. | Golu standing on the pavement |
| Optimist/Follower | Proximate to whoever is speaking. Mobile — shifts between characters. Low to ground. | Biscuit between Golu and Bandu |
| Tired/Withdrawn | Corner or wall. Compressed shape (C-curve, curled). Not facing the group. Minimal movement. | Chiku against the wall |
| Authority | Elevated or centered. Does not move toward others — others move toward them. Minimal gestures. | Bandu on the step |
| Mediator | Positioned between two other characters. Body angled to face both. | Biscuit between Golu and Bandu |
| Outsider | At far distance from group. Body oriented away. Does not enter the group's space. | Scraps at edge, facing street |

## 5. GROUP FORMATIONS

### 5.1 PRINCIPLE

Every group scene has a formation. The formation is the spatial arrangement of all characters relative to each other and the environment.

Formations are not random. They are determined by:
- The scene's social dynamics (who leads, who follows, who conflicts)
- The environment's affordances (what spaces are available)
- Each character's spatial archetype
- The narrative beat

### 5.2 FORMATION TYPES

#### Circle

All characters face inward at roughly equal distances. No elevation differential.

```
    A
  B   E
    C
    D
```

**Meaning:** Equality, discussion, democracy. No clear hierarchy. All participants are present.
**Use when:** A group is conferring as equals, or when hierarchy is intentionally absent.
**Limitation:** Dogs do not naturally form inward-facing circles. A circle forces every character to expose their back to the outside. This is a trusting urban human formation, not a street dog formation.
**When to avoid:** Any scene where threat awareness or hierarchy matters.

#### V-Formation

A leader at the point, others arrayed behind at angles.

```
    A
   B C
  D   E
```

**Meaning:** Movement, direction, leadership. The group is following a leader.
**Use when:** The group is traveling, being led, or the leader is clearly established.
**Variation:** A tight V (close proximity) = strong cohesion. A loose V = casual following, less urgency.

#### Cluster

Characters grouped close together, no clear geometric arrangement.

```
  A B
   C
  D E
```

**Meaning:** Intimacy, shared focus, informality. The group is focused on something small (food, an object, a conversation).
**Use when:** The group shares attention on one thing. No hierarchy needs to be expressed.

#### Scatter

Characters positioned at various points in the environment, not grouped.

```
A
        B
  C
              D
      E
```

**Meaning:** Disconnection, searching, danger, aftermath. The group has broken formation.
**Use when:** Characters are searching for something, have been scattered by danger, or are not operating as a unit.

#### Stacked

Characters arranged along a vertical axis (elevation differential).

```
   A (step)
  B  C (ground)
   D
  E (lower pavement)
```

**Meaning:** Explicit hierarchy. High status at the top, low status at the bottom.
**Use when:** Authority dynamics are the primary spatial statement.

#### Threshold

Characters arranged at a boundary — a doorway, a step, a street edge.

```
          [INTERIOR]
         A
      B     C
  D
     [EXTERIOR]
```

**Meaning:** Transition, liminality, before/after. Some characters are positioned to enter, others to stay, others to guard the boundary.
**Use when:** A group is about to move, or some members are excluded from a space.

### 5.3 FORMATION TRANSITIONS

A scene may begin in one formation and end in another. The transition communicates narrative change.

| Transition | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Scatter → Cluster | Strangers gathering, team assembling, safety found |
| Cluster → V-Formation | Discussion ended, action begins, leader selected |
| Circle → Scatter | Conflict, disagreement, danger interrupts |
| V-Formation → Threshold | The group arrives where it was going |
| Cluster → Stacked | Authority asserted, hierarchy established |
| V-Formation → Scatter | Ambush, panic, pursuit |

## 6. BLOCKING

### 6.1 PRINCIPLE

Blocking is how characters move through space during a scene.

Spatial Staging defines the starting formation, the ending formation, and the blocking that connects them. The Camera Department observes the blocking. The Writer motivates the blocking through action.

Blocking always answers: **"Why does this character move now, in this direction, at this speed?"**

### 6.2 BLOCKING ELEMENTS

| Element | Definition |
|---|---|
| Movement trigger | What causes the character to move (a sound, a line of dialogue, an arrival, a decision) |
| Path | The trajectory through space (straight, curved, zigzag, circling) |
| Speed | Walking, trotting, running, bolting, creeping |
| Quality | Confident, hesitant, urgent, casual, dramatic |
| Target | Where the character is going (another character, a prop, an exit, a position) |
| Interruption | What stops or changes the movement |

### 6.3 BLOCKING RULES

- Every movement must have a trigger. Nothing moves because "it was time to move."
- A character moving toward another character is either approaching (positive) or confronting (negative). The quality of movement distinguishes them.
- A character moving away from another is either departing (neutral), retreating (negative), or leading (positive if others follow).
- When a character changes formation (e.g., moves from edge to center), the other characters must react — heads turn, bodies shift, space opens or closes.
- The character who moves last in a scene is the dominant spatial figure. Their movement resolves the formation.

### 6.4 BLOCKING EXIT PATTERNS

When a group exits together:

| Pattern | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Leader exits first, others follow in rank | Ordered departure. The leader decides when the group moves. |
| Leader exits last, after others | Protective departure. The leader ensures everyone is safe before leaving. |
| Leader exits alone, group follows in scatter | Chaos. The leader has not communicated the exit — others scramble to follow. |
| The most enthusiastic exits first, others follow | The excited one sets the direction, the leader ratifies by following. (Biscuit bounces ahead, Scraps follows.) |

## 7. ENVIRONMENTAL AFFORDANCES

### 7.1 PRINCIPLE

Every environment contains spatial affordances — features that invite or constrain character positioning. Spatial Staging must identify every affordance and decide whether each character uses it.

### 7.2 AFFORDANCE CATALOG

| Affordance | Spatial Opportunity | Typical Occupant |
|---|---|---|
| Step | Elevation, authority, safe sitting (back against wall) | The senior, the leader |
| Wall | Back protection, shelter, lean surface, boundary | The tired, the vulnerable, the guard (back to wall) |
| Corner (90°) | Maximum shelter, two-sided protection, containment | The most vulnerable, the exhausted, the wounded |
| Open space | Display, movement, visibility, center stage | The loud, the expressive, the performer |
| Edge/drop-off | Guard position, departure readiness, threat monitoring | The protector, the sentry |
| Pillar/post | Cover, partial concealment, lean surface, territorial marker | The observer, the suspicious |
| Threshold/doorway | Liminal position, partial entry/exit, transition | The hesitant, the arriving, the departing |
| Elevated surface | Overlook, authority, separation | The leader, the scout |
| Enclosed space (box, alcove, gap) | Complete shelter, concealment, safety | The hiding, the injured, the sleeping |
| Narrow passage | Single-file movement, constraint, vulnerability | Any character in transit |

### 7.3 AFFORDANCE RULES

- Every affordance that exists in the environment must be considered. An unused affordance is a missed spatial statement.
- A character who ignores an affordance is making a choice. A tired character near a wall who does not use the wall is choosing exposure over comfort — a narrative signal.
- When multiple characters could claim the same affordance, the highest-ranking character gets it. If a lower-ranking character claims it, that is a challenge to hierarchy.
- Affordances that appear in one scene and disappear in the next (a step that was there but is now absent) must be explained by environment change.

## 8. SIGHTLINES

### 8.1 PRINCIPLE

Spatial Staging determines what each character can see from their position. Sightlines influence behavior — a character who sees a threat behaves differently than one who does not.

### 8.2 CHARACTER SIGHTLINE

Character Sightline is the set of information visible from a character's eye position at their current orientation. It is what the character can perceive, not what the camera sees.

The Camera Department (Book III) determines what the *audience* observes.
Spatial Staging determines what each *character* observes.

These are different. A character may see something the audience does not (the camera is looking elsewhere). The audience may see something the character does not (dramatic irony).

### 8.3 SIGHTLINE RULES

- Spatial Staging must determine each character's sightline — what they can see based on position and head orientation.
- When a character's sightline contains a threat or point of interest that other characters cannot see, the audience should read it through the character's behavior (ear orientation, head turn, body tension).
- When a character is positioned so they cannot see a critical event, that is a deliberate choice. They become vulnerable or ignorant for narrative reasons.
- Contested sightlines (two characters looking at the same point from different positions) create shared attention or competition.
- Blocked sightlines (a character's view is occluded by geometry or another character) create dependency — the character must trust what they cannot see.

### 8.4 AUDIENCE AWARENESS

Spatial Staging must flag character sightlines that affect the audience's understanding:
- What a character sees and the audience does not: creates mystery (the character reacts to something invisible).
- What the audience sees and a character does not: creates dramatic irony (we know the threat before they do).
- What both see: creates shared experience.
- What neither sees: creates potential (unknown unknowns).

## 9. SPATIAL CONTINUITY

### 9.1 PRINCIPLE

Space persists. The arrangement of characters at the end of one scene must be consistent with the beginning of the next, unless time has passed or characters have moved.

### 9.2 CONTINUITY RULES

- Spatial Staging reads the previous scene's spatial_staging_specification to inherit character positions and orientations.
- If the new scene occurs in the same location with no time gap, characters begin in the same positions they ended the previous scene.
- If time has passed, characters may have moved, but the movement must be explainable (they walked, they rested, they left and returned).
- Formation changes between scenes require narrative justification.

### 9.3 PERSISTENT SPATIAL CLAIMS

- A character who claimed an affordance (e.g., the step) in a previous scene will default to that same spot unless the narrative requires them to move.
- A character who yielded territory (moved from center to edge) in a previous scene will remain at the edge unless they reclaim their position.

## 11. EXECUTION POSITION

Spatial Staging works after the Production Designer (who builds the environment) and before the Character Supervisor (who verifies continuity).

### 11.1 EXECUTION ORDER

The full execution order is defined in Book I, Section 11 (Execution Order). Spatial Staging works after the Production Designer (who builds the environment) and before the Character Supervisor (who verifies continuity).

Spatial Staging reads:
- `scene_specification` (Writer)
- `environment_specification` (Book X)
- `cinematic_language_specification` (Book IV)
- `world_state` (Book II)
- `character_states` (Book II)

Spatial Staging produces:
- `spatial_staging_specification`

### 11.2 WHY SPATIAL STAGING COMES AFTER PRODUCTION DESIGNER

Spatial Staging must know what the environment contains before deciding where characters go. The Production Designer defines the step, the wall, the corner, the open pavement. Spatial Staging assigns characters to those affordances.

### 11.3 WHY SPATIAL STAGING COMES BEFORE CHARACTER SUPERVISOR

The Character Supervisor verifies that characters are consistent with their definitions and previous states. Spatial Staging determines where they are and how they move. The Character Supervisor needs this information to verify that the spatial arrangement does not violate character traits (e.g., a protective character being placed where they cannot see threats) or continuity (e.g., a character being in two places at once).

## 12. RELATIONSHIP TO OTHER DEPARTMENTS

### 12.1 SPATIAL STAGING AND PRODUCTION DESIGNER

The Production Designer provides the affordances. Spatial Staging assigns them to characters. Neither can work without the other. The Production Designer must ensure every scene has sufficient affordances for the number of characters present. A scene with five characters and one flat empty space cannot be staged.

### 12.2 SPATIAL STAGING AND WRITER

The Writer's scene_specification contains actions and dialogue. Spatial Staging reads these to determine blocking — when a character moves, where they go, what triggers the movement. The Writer should not specify blocking. The Writer specifies what happens; Spatial Staging specifies where it happens in physical space.

### 12.3 SPATIAL STAGING AND CAMERA

The Camera Department reads spatial_staging_specification to determine where to position the observing camera. Spatial Staging tells the Camera where characters are, how they are oriented, what their sightlines are. The Camera decides how to observe that arrangement. Spatial Staging may also include flags that tell the Camera what must be visible to convey the spatial statement.

### 12.4 SPATIAL STAGING AND CINEMATIC LANGUAGE

The Cinematic Language department (Book IV) defines concepts like Isolation, Authority, Fear. Spatial Staging implements these concepts physically — Isolation is a character at far distance from the group, Authority is a character on elevated ground. Each concept from Book IV should have a spatial implementation in Book VII.
