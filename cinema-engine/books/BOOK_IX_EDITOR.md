# CINEMA SKILL — BOOK IX: THE EDITOR

> This document extends Books I through VIII.
> Everything written here obeys the Constitution.
> No principle in this document may violate any previous book.
> This book becomes the permanent editorial authority of Cinema.
> The Editor executes intention. The Editor never invents intention.

---

## 1. PURPOSE

The Editor does not cut film.

The Editor controls time.

The Editor determines how long the audience spends with each image, how they move between images, and what rhythm accumulates across the sequence. The Editor does not create images — the Camera creates them. The Editor decides which images the audience sees, in what order, for how long, and how they connect.

The Editor is the temporal authority of Cinema.

---

## 2. FUNDAMENTAL PHILOSOPHY

Editing is not the arrangement of shots.
Editing is the control of time and information.

Every cut is a decision about:
- **When** the audience receives information
- **How long** they have to process it
- **What they carry** from one moment to the next
- **What they leave behind**

The Editor does not serve the Camera. The Editor does not serve the Sound department. The Editor serves the audience's experience of time.

A cut that does not change the audience's understanding of time is not editing. It is interruption.

---

## 3. THE SIX CRITERIA OF A GOOD CUT

Walter Murch, the celebrated editor of Apocalypse Now, The English Patient, and Cold Mountain, defined six criteria for evaluating any cut. They are presented here as a prioritized hierarchy.

### 3.1 THE HIERARCHY

```
1. EMOTION       — (worth 50%) — Is the cut true to the emotion of the moment?
2. STORY         — (worth 20%) — Does the cut advance the story?
3. RHYTHM        — (worth 10%) — Does the cut occur at a rhythmically right moment?
4. EYE-TRACE     — (worth 10%) — Does the cut respect where the audience is looking?
5. PLANARITY     — (worth 5%)  — Does the cut respect the 2D screen geography?
6. SPATIAL       — (worth 5%)  — Does the cut respect 3D continuity of space?
```

**Rule:** The top of the list (emotion) is worth more than all five criteria below it combined. If a cut serves the emotion but breaks spatial continuity, it is a good cut. If a cut preserves spatial continuity but damages the emotion, it is a bad cut.

### 3.2 APPLYING THE HIERARCHY

When choosing between shots or timing a cut:

1. First ask: Does this serve the **emotion** the Director intended?
2. Then ask: Does this advance the **story**?
3. Then ask: Is the **rhythm** right?
4. Then verify: Does the **eye-trace** work — will the audience's gaze land naturally in the new shot?
5. Then check: Is the **planarity** (screen direction, 180-degree line) maintained?
6. Finally: Is the **spatial continuity** preserved?

If you must sacrifice something, sacrifice from the bottom up. Never sacrifice emotion for spatial continuity.

### 3.3 EMBODIED SIMULATION

Research in cognitive film studies (Gallese & Guerra 2012, Pearlman 2016) shows that editors' embodied simulation (ES) responses — their physical, intuitive feeling of rightness — correlate with audience engagement. When an edit "feels right" to an experienced editor, it is because their body has simulated the emotional and physical response the audience will have.

Editors should trust their embodied response as a valid data point, but verify it against the hierarchy above. Intuition is trained expertise, not guesswork.

## 4. RESPONSIBILITY

The Editor owns only:

- **Shot selection** — which shots from the camera_specification are used
- **Shot duration** — how long each shot is held
- **Shot order** — the sequence in which shots appear
- **Transitions** — how shots connect (cut, dissolve, wipe, match cut, etc.)
- **Pacing** — the speed at which information is delivered
- **Rhythm** — the pattern of shot lengths and transitions
- **Information release over time** — when the audience learns what
- **Temporal compression and expansion** — how time is stretched or condensed
- **J-cuts and L-cuts** — audio leading or following video across transitions
- **Continuity editing** — maintaining spatial and temporal coherence

Nothing else.

---

## 5. WHAT THE EDITOR NEVER OWNS

- Story or narrative purpose (Director)
- Visual grammar or symbolism (Cinematic Language)
- Camera position or movement (Camera)
- Character behaviour or dialogue (Writer)
- Environment design or props (Production Designer)
- Character positions in space (Spatial Staging)
- Lighting (Book VI)
- Sound design (Book VIII)
- Render prompts or renderer settings (Prompt Compiler)

The Editor reads the documents from previous departments and translates their intent into temporal structure. The Editor does not originate intent.

## 6. EDITING GRAMMAR

### 6.1 PRINCIPLE

Editing Grammar is a dictionary. Each entry maps an abstract concept to its temporal expression.

These are not technical specifications. These are descriptions of how editing communicates each idea through time.

### 6.2 THE DICTIONARY

#### CUT

The basic transition. Instantaneous. No time passes between shots. The cut is invisible when it follows a motivated action (a character looks, the camera follows their gaze). The cut is visible when it creates discontinuity (jump cut, time jump). The cut is violent when it interrupts (cut to black, cut to shock). The cut is gentle when it flows (matching action, matching composition).

#### DISSOLVE

Time passes. A dissolve is the visual equivalent of "and then..." It connects two moments that are separated by time. Short dissolve = brief time gap. Long dissolve = significant time passage. Overlapping images in dissolve = memory, dream, or parallel action.

#### MATCH CUT

A cut that connects two shots through visual or auditory similarity. The match creates a bridge between unrelated spaces or times. A match on shape (round object to round object). A match on action (door closing to another door closing). A match on sound (a word to an echo). The match cut is the Editor's most powerful tool for making connections the audience feels before they understand.

#### WIPE

A transition that moves across the frame, replacing one image with another. Wipes are directional — left to right, top to bottom, circular. A wipe implies force, energy, or stylistic boldness. Wipes feel less natural than cuts or dissolves because they call attention to themselves.

#### HOLD

The absence of a transition. The shot continues without cutting. A hold is a decision to let the audience stay with one image. Short hold = observation, passing moment. Long hold = contemplation, tension, weight. Very long hold = the audience becomes uncomfortable — which may be the intent.

#### JUMP CUT

A cut between two shots of the same subject with a small temporal gap. The subject changes position slightly. The jump cut calls attention to time. It is disruptive, energetic, modern. It breaks classical continuity. It says: time matters, and we are skipping the boring parts.

#### INSERT

A brief shot of a detail (a hand, an object, a reaction) inserted into a longer sequence. An insert is information delivery — the audience needs to see this detail now, not later. The insert is the Editor's way of controlling attention at the micro level.

#### REACTION SHOT

A shot of a character responding to an event. The reaction shot does not show the event — it shows the effect of the event. The Editor places the reaction shot after the event to let the audience process through the character. Or before the event to create anticipation.

#### POV CUT

A cut from a character looking to what they see, or from what they see back to the character. The POV cut creates identification — the audience sees through the character's eyes. The Editor controls when the audience shares the character's perspective and when they are separated from it.

#### CROSS-CUTTING / PARALLEL EDITING

Cutting between two or more simultaneous actions happening in different locations. Cross-cutting creates comparison, contrast, tension, or connection. The Editor controls the rhythm of the cross-cut — how long we spend with each action before switching. Faster cross-cutting = higher tension. Slower cross-cutting = contemplation.

#### MONTAGE

A sequence of brief shots compressed into a short time, usually with music. Montage covers time, develops character, or establishes routine. The Editor controls the montage's rhythm — how many shots, how long each, what transitions between them.

## 7. SHOT DURATION PHILOSOPHY

### 7.1 PRINCIPLE

How long a shot is held determines how the audience processes it.

### 7.2 DURATION CATEGORIES

| Duration | Audience Experience | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Under 1 second | Subliminal, flash, visceral | Shock, violence, memory fragments, montage |
| 1-2 seconds | Brief, passing, informational | Standard dialogue coverage, action beats, reactions |
| 3-5 seconds | Standard, comfortable, processed | Most narrative shots, dialogue exchanges, action |
| 6-10 seconds | Contemplative, weighted, significant | Emotional moments, reveals, important information |
| 11-20 seconds | Demanding, tense, uncomfortable | Tension building, the audience wants to look away |
| Over 20 seconds | Monumental, transcendent, unbearable | Death, birth, transcendence, the infinite |

### 7.3 DURATION RULES

- The first shot of a scene should be held long enough for the audience to understand where they are. A cut too soon creates confusion.
- The last shot of a scene should be held long enough for the audience to feel the scene's ending. A cut too soon feels abrupt; too late feels indulgent.
- Close-ups can be shorter than wide shots because they contain less information to process.
- Complex shots (many elements, deep focus, multiple actions) need longer holds.
- Shots with significant movement need longer holds — the audience must see the movement complete.
- Reaction shots need precise timing. Too short = the audience misses the reaction. Too long = the reaction becomes performative.

## 8. RHYTHM

### 8.1 PRINCIPLE

Rhythm is the pattern of shot durations and transitions across a scene or sequence.

### 8.2 PEARLMAN'S RHYTHM FRAMEWORK

Karen Pearlman, in *Cutting Rhythms* (2009/2016), defines editing rhythm as the interaction of three primary elements:

**Time** — the duration of shots, the tempo of cutting, the relationship between screen time and story time. Time is the quantitative axis of rhythm.

**Energy** — the intensity within shots and across cuts. A static dialogue scene has low energy. A chase scene has high energy. Energy is created through movement within the frame (actors, camera), pacing of dialogue, and emotional intensity.

**Movement** — the physical motion within and between shots. This includes character movement, camera movement, and the movement created by cutting itself. Movement is the qualitative shape of rhythm.

Pearlman also defines three levels of rhythmic structure:

| Level | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Timing** | When a cut occurs relative to the action in the frame | Cutting on a gesture, on a word, on a breath |
| **Pacing** | The overall speed and tempo of a sequence | Slow pacing for reflection, fast pacing for action |
| **Trajectory phrasing** | The shape of a sequence: build, sustain, release, or any combination | A scene that builds tension to a climax then releases |

**Rule:** Trajectory phrasing is the dominant level. Before choosing timing or pacing, the Editor must know the trajectory phrase — is this scene building, sustaining, or releasing? The trajectory phrase determines all rhythmic decisions.

### 8.3 RHYTHM PATTERNS

| Pattern | Description | Emotional Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Steady | Consistent shot lengths, consistent transitions | Calm, routine, baseline, stability |
| Accelerating | Shots get shorter, cuts get faster | Building tension, urgency, panic, excitement |
| Decelerating | Shots get longer, cuts slow down | Release, reflection, grief, aftermath |
| Broken | Steady rhythm interrupted by one very long or very short shot | Shock, surprise, revelation, the exception that proves the rule |
| Contrapuntal | Visual rhythm contradicts emotional content | Calm editing of chaos (unsettling), frantic editing of stillness (anxious) |
| Syncopated | Irregular rhythm — long, short, medium, short, long | Jazz-like energy, unpredictability, modern feel |
| Staccato | Very short shots, rapid cuts, no dissolves | Violence, chaos, montage, information overload |
| Legato | Long shots, slow dissolves, minimal cutting | Dream, memory, romance, contemplation, classical beauty |

### 8.4 RHYTHM AND SOUND

The Editor collaborates with the Sound department (Book VIII) on rhythm:
- Music rhythm determines editing rhythm. Cuts on beats, cuts on silence, cuts on crescendos.
- Sound effects provide natural cut points. A door slam, an impact, a shout — these are moments the Editor can cut on.
- Dialogue rhythm determines shot duration. A long speech = longer shots. Rapid back-and-forth = shorter shots.
- Silence provides natural pause points. The Editor should not cut during a meaningful silence.

## 9. INFORMATION RELEASE OVER TIME

### 9.1 PRINCIPLE

The Editor controls when the audience receives information, not just what information they receive.

The Director decides what the audience should learn (Book V, Information Control). The Editor decides when they learn it.

### 9.2 TEMPORAL INFORMATION STRATEGIES

| Strategy | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate | Information is shown in the first shot | Wide shot establishing the location |
| Delayed | Information is withheld until a later shot | Close-up on an object reveals its significance |
| Gradual | Information is revealed progressively across multiple shots | A character's emotional state revealed through reaction shots |
| Sudden | Information arrives in one shot after being absent | Cut to a new location, cut to a new character |
| Repetition | Information is shown multiple times | Cut back to the same detail, the same reaction |
| Parallel | Information is shown in cross-cutting between two actions | Character A does X while Character B does Y |

### 9.3 RULES

- Never reveal critical information in a shot that is too short to process it. The audience will miss it.
- Never hold a shot with no new information for too long. The audience will become bored unless the boredom is intentional.
- When two pieces of information must be connected, the Editor places them close together in time. When they must be separated, the Editor places them far apart.
- The Editor can create dramatic irony by placing information the audience knows but the character does not in shots that the character cannot see.

## 10. CONTINUITY EDITING

### 10.1 PRINCIPLE

Continuity editing maintains the illusion of continuous space and time across cuts.

### 10.2 CONTINUITY RULES

#### 180-Degree Rule

An imaginary line runs through the scene between the primary subjects. The camera must stay on one side of this line. Crossing the line reverses screen direction and disorients the audience. The Editor must ensure the Camera department's shots respect the established line, or the Editor must use a shot on the line (a neutral shot) to cross safely.

#### Eyeline Match

When a character looks at something off-screen, the next shot should show what they are looking at from approximately their eye level and angle. The Editor ensures the eyeline connects logically across the cut.

#### Action Match

When a character performs an action that continues across a cut (picking up an object, walking through a door), the action must continue smoothly across the cut. The Editor places the cut at the moment of action to hide the transition.

#### Screen Direction

Characters and objects must maintain consistent left-to-right or right-to-left movement across cuts. If a character moves left-to-right in one shot, they must continue moving left-to-right in the next shot (unless the cut shows them turning or changing direction). The Editor ensures screen direction is maintained or intentionally broken.

## 11. TRANSITION PHILOSOPHY

### 11.1 PRINCIPLE

Every transition is a statement about time.

### 11.2 TRANSITION MEANINGS

| Transition | Temporal Statement | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Hard cut | Time is continuous. We are in the same moment. | Standard narrative progression, action continuity |
| Dissolve | Time has passed. We are in a new moment. | Time jumps, memory, dreams, parallel action |
| Fade to black | Time has ended. This chapter closes. | Scene endings, acts, death, sleep |
| Fade from black | Time is beginning. We are entering. | Scene openings, awakening, new beginnings |
| Match cut | Time and space are connected through similarity. | Thematic connections, shape rhymes, visual poetry |
| Wipe | Force is applied to time. Time moves directionally. | Energy, style, comedy, period feel |
| Jump cut | Time is broken. We are skipping. | Modern energy, urgency, documentary feel |
| Split screen | Time is simultaneous. Two moments exist together. | Parallel action, comparison, phone calls |

## 12. J-CUTS AND L-CUTS

### 12.1 PRINCIPLE

Audio and video do not need to transition simultaneously. Separating them creates richer temporal experience.

### 12.2 DEFINITIONS

| Type | Video | Audio | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| J-cut | Shot A continues | Sound from Shot B begins early | Anticipation — we hear the next thing before we see it |
| L-cut | Shot A ends early | Sound from Shot A continues into Shot B | Memory — we leave the image but carry its sound |

### 12.3 RULES

- J-cuts create forward momentum. The audience is pulled into the next shot by its sound.
- L-cuts create lingering emotion. The audience carries the feeling of the previous shot into the new one.
- Both techniques require the Editor to work closely with the Sound department (Book VIII).
- Overused, J-cuts and L-cuts become a style. Used selectively, they are powerful narrative tools.

## 14. EXECUTION POSITION

The Editor works after Sound (which provides audio design and natural cut points) and before Producer (who merges all documents).

### 14.1 EXECUTION ORDER

The full execution order is defined in Book I, Section 11 (Execution Order). The Editor works after Sound (which provides audio design and natural cut points) and before the Producer (who merges all documents).

The Editor reads:
- `camera_specification` (Book III)
- `sound_specification` (Book VIII)
- `cinematic_language_specification` (Book IV)
- `director_report` (Book V)
- `world_state` (Book II)

The Editor produces:
- `edit_decision_list`

### 14.2 WHY EDITOR COMES AFTER SOUND

The Editor determines where cuts occur. Sound design provides natural cut points — door slams, musical crescendos, silence breaks. The Editor needs the sound_specification to make informed decisions about timing. A cut on a musical beat is different from a cut on silence. The Editor cannot determine rhythm without knowing the audio rhythm.

### 14.3 WHY EDITOR COMES BEFORE PRODUCER

The Producer merges all documents. The Editor's edit_decision_list is the final department output before merging. It synthesizes the visual (Camera), audio (Sound), and temporal decisions into a unified timeline.

### 16.3 INVARIANT

This specification contains:
- No camera positions (those belong to Camera).
- No lighting instructions.
- No renderer language.
- No sound design details (those belong to Sound).
- No dialogue content.

It contains only: shot selection, shot duration, transitions, rhythm patterns, temporal structure, continuity notes, and information release timing. The Camera department produced the shots. The Editor curates them into time.

## 17. SCOPE BOUNDARY

This book defines ONLY the Editor department.

It does NOT define:
- Camera positions, lenses, or movement
- Lighting systems
- Render prompts or renderer syntax
- Spatial staging
- Sound design details
- Character behaviour or dialogue content

Those belong to their respective books.

## 18. CONSTITUTIONAL COMPLIANCE

This book complies with all applicable principles from Books I through VIII: departments are isolated, the World is the single source of truth, the Camera observes but does not create meaning, Cinematic Language translates intent into visual behaviour, the Director decides why a scene exists, Lighting reveals information, Spatial Staging determines character positions, and Sound is information. No principle in this document violates the Constitution or any previous book.
