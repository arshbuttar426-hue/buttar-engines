# CINEMA SKILL — BOOK VI: LIGHTING

> This document extends Books I through V.
> Everything written here obeys the Constitution.
> No principle in this document may violate any previous book.
> This book becomes the permanent Lighting authority of Cinema.
> Lighting executes intention. Lighting never invents intention.

---

## 1. PURPOSE

Lighting does not decorate a scene.

Lighting reveals information.
Lighting hides information.
Lighting directs attention.
Lighting establishes atmosphere.
Lighting supports the Director's intention.

Lighting never creates story.
Lighting never changes emotion.
Lighting only expresses what previous departments have already decided.

The Director decides what the audience should feel. The Cinematic Language department decides what visual behaviour expresses that feeling. The Camera department decides what is observable. Lighting decides how clearly it is observable, what is illuminated, and what is withheld in darkness.

---

## 2. FUNDAMENTAL PHILOSOPHY

Light is information.
Darkness is withheld information.

Every light source must justify its existence.
Every shadow must justify its existence.

Nothing is illuminated without narrative purpose.
Nothing remains hidden without narrative purpose.

A brightly lit room is not "good lighting." A brightly lit room is a statement: everything in this room matters and nothing is hidden. That statement must serve the Director's intent. If the Director's intent requires the audience to suspect, to doubt, to search — then full illumination betrays the intent.

Lighting is the permission the audience receives to see. Every adjustment to that permission is a narrative act.

---

## 3. RESPONSIBILITY

Lighting owns only:

- **Natural light** — sun, sky, moon, starlight, lightning
- **Artificial light** — lamps, streetlights, headlights, screens, neon
- **Practical lights** — light sources visible within the scene that characters could interact with (a candle, a lantern, a phone screen)
- **Exposure** — the overall level of illumination that determines how much the audience can perceive
- **Shadow structure** — where shadows fall, how deep they are, what they obscure, what they suggest
- **Contrast** — the ratio between light and dark, which determines visual tension
- **Light hierarchy** — which light sources dominate, which support, which are subordinate
- **Light direction** — where light originates and how it travels through the space
- **Light quality** — hard (sharp, directional) vs soft (diffused, scattered), and the spectrum between
- **Light continuity** — the persistence of lighting states across scenes, changed only by cause
- **Atmospheric visibility** — how atmosphere (dust, fog, rain, mist) modifies the behaviour of light in the space

Nothing else.

---

## 4. WHAT LIGHTING NEVER OWNS

- Story or narrative purpose (Director)
- Visual grammar or symbolism (Cinematic Language)
- Camera position or movement (Camera)
- Character behaviour or dialogue (Writer)
- Environment design or props (Production Designer)
- Sound (Book VIII)
- Editing rhythm (Book IX — Editor)
- Render prompts or renderer settings (Book XIII — Prompt Compiler)
- Color grading or LUTs (future post-production concerns)
- Film stock or renderer color science

Lighting reads the documents from previous departments and translates their intent into illumination. It does not originate intent.

---

## 5. LIGHT SOURCES

### 5.1 CATEGORIES

Every light source belongs to one category:

| Category | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Natural | Light from natural celestial or atmospheric sources | Sun, sky, moon, starlight, lightning, aurora |
| Artificial | Light from manufactured sources not visible in the scene | Off-screen lamp, streetlight from outside, overhead fixture not in frame |
| Practical | Light from sources visible within the scene that characters could interact with | Candle, lantern, phone screen, fireplace, torch, neon sign, desk lamp |
| Environmental | Light reflected or scattered by the environment | Bounce light from a wall, water reflection, snow scatter, floor bounce |
| Reflected | Light redirected by a specific reflective surface | Mirror, window, puddle, metallic surface |
| Motivated | Light that has no visible source but is justified by the environment | A warm glow that implies an off-screen fire, a cool wash that implies moonlight through an unseen window |

### 5.2 NARRATIVE JUSTIFICATION

Each source must have narrative justification. A light source exists because:
- The environment logically contains it (a street has streetlights).
- The story requires it (the Director needs the audience to see something).
- The emotional intent demands it (the Cinematic Language calls for warmth or coldness).
- The atmosphere requires it (fog needs a light source to become visible).

A light source that exists only because "it looks good" has no justification. Remove it.

### 5.3 SOURCE HIERARCHY

Within a scene, light sources are organized hierarchically:
- **Key source** — the dominant light that establishes the primary illumination and direction.
- **Fill source** — the secondary light that softens shadows and reveals what the key obscures.
- **Rim/edge source** — the light that separates subjects from the background.
- **Atmospheric source** — light that interacts with atmosphere to create visible beams, haze, or scatter.
- **Accent source** — small, targeted light that highlights a specific detail the audience should notice.

The hierarchy is determined by narrative priority, not by technical convention.

## 6. INFORMATION HIERARCHY

### 6.1 PRINCIPLE

Lighting determines what the audience is permitted to see and how clearly.

### 6.2 VISIBILITY TIERS

Lighting organizes the scene into visibility tiers:

| Tier | Meaning | Lighting Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Immediately visible** | The audience perceives this without effort. | Fully illuminated, high contrast against surroundings, light directs attention here. |
| **Requires observation** | The audience can see this but must look for it. | Partially lit, lower contrast, exists in the periphery or in shadow edges. |
| **Hidden** | The audience cannot see this but it exists in the space. | In shadow, unlit, or occluded by shadow structure. Withheld for narrative purpose. |
| **Intentionally impossible** | The audience cannot see this and should not be able to. | In deep shadow or total darkness. The lighting explicitly denies observation. |

### 6.3 RULE

The visibility tiers must align with the Director's information control (Book V) and the Cinematic Language department's attention specification (Book IV).

- What the Director says the audience **must never miss** → immediately visible.
- What the Director says the audience **should notice** → requires observation (available but not forced).
- What the Director says the audience **should miss** → hidden (present but unlit or in shadow).
- What the Director says is **intentionally withheld** → intentionally impossible (darkness).

If the lighting tiers contradict the Director's information control, the Producer resolves the conflict.

## 7. SHADOW PHILOSOPHY

### 7.1 PRINCIPLE

Shadows are active storytelling devices. They are not the absence of light. They are the presence of withheld information.

### 7.2 SHADOW FUNCTIONS

Shadows may:

- **Hide** — a character or object is concealed in shadow. The audience knows something is there but cannot see it.
- **Suggest** — a shadow implies the presence of something outside the frame or behind an obstacle. The shadow is visible; its source is not.
- **Delay** — a subject begins in shadow and emerges into light. The delay creates anticipation.
- **Separate** — shadow divides the frame into lit and unlit zones, separating characters or spaces emotionally.
- **Protect** — a character in shadow is safe from observation by others in the scene. Shadow as refuge.
- **Reveal** — a shadow cast by an unseen object reveals that object's shape or presence without showing the object itself.

### 7.3 RULE

Shadows are never accidental. Every shadow in a scene exists because the narrative requires it to hide, suggest, delay, separate, protect, or reveal.

A shadow that exists only because "it looks dramatic" without narrative justification violates this principle.

### 7.4 SHADOW DEPTH

Shadow depth communicates:
- **Deep shadow** — total or near-total darkness. The audience cannot perceive what is within. Maximum withholding.
- **Moderate shadow** — the audience can perceive shapes and movement but not detail. Tension and suspicion.
- **Soft shadow** — the audience can perceive most detail but the area is dimmer than the surroundings. Subtle de-emphasis.

The depth is chosen based on what information the Director wants withheld and how strongly.

## 8. LIGHT CONTINUITY

### 8.1 PRINCIPLE

Lighting belongs to the World (Book II). It cannot randomly change.

Lighting state is part of the World's persistent state. The World Engine tracks:
- Current light sources active in each location
- Current light quality (natural light changes as time passes)
- Current shadow structure (determined by light source positions and geometry)
- Current atmospheric conditions (affecting light behaviour)

### 8.2 CHANGE REQUIRES CAUSE

Lighting changes only when causally justified:

| Cause | Effect |
|---|---|
| Power loss | Artificial and practical lights extinguish. Natural light remains. |
| Cloud movement | Natural light quality shifts (hard sun → soft overcast). Direction may change. |
| Sunset / sunrise | Natural light color temperature, direction, and intensity shift over time. |
| Fire | New practical light source. Dynamic, warm, flickering. Creates moving shadows. |
| Explosion | Brief intense flash. May extinguish or damage other light sources. |
| Broken bulb | A practical or artificial source goes dark. Shadows restructure. |
| Opened door | New light spills into the space from the adjacent room or exterior. |
| Character action | A character turns on a lamp, blows out a candle, opens shutters. |
| Time passage | Natural light shifts according to the time advancement in the World State. |

### 8.3 RULE

Never change lighting because it "looks cinematic." Lighting changes because something in the world caused it. If the Director needs a different lighting state for emotional purposes, the cause must be written into the scene (a character opens a door, the sun sets, a bulb breaks).

The Lighting department does not invent causes. It reads causes from the scene_specification and environment_specification and translates them into lighting changes.

## 9. ATMOSPHERE

### 9.1 PRINCIPLE

Atmosphere modifies visibility. It is not decoration.

### 9.2 ATMOSPHERIC ELEMENTS

| Element | Effect on Light |
|---|---|
| Dust | Scatters light beams, makes light paths visible (volumetric), softens distant visibility. |
| Fog | Diffuses light broadly, reduces contrast at distance, creates depth-based visibility falloff. |
| Smoke | Creates dense, moving light obstructions. Colored by light sources it passes through. |
| Rain | Refracts and reflects light, creates specular highlights on surfaces, reduces overall visibility. |
| Steam | Localized diffusion, softens light in specific areas, dynamic and moving. |
| Mist | Gentle, even reduction of distant visibility. Softens all edges. |
| Humidity | Subtle haze. Slightly softens contrast. May not be visible but affects light quality. |
| Particles | Dust motes, debris, pollen — catch light individually, create visible points of scattered light. |

### 9.3 RULE

Atmosphere never exists purely for aesthetics. Atmosphere exists because:
- The environment logically produces it (rain creates mist, a fire creates smoke).
- The story establishes it (a factory has steam, a dusty road has suspended particles).
- The Cinematic Language requires it (suspicion calls for obscured visibility, wonder calls for visible light beams).

### 9.4 ATMOSPHERE AND OBSERVATION

Atmosphere affects observation (Book III). The Camera department's Visibility Solver must account for atmospheric conditions when determining what is visible, partially visible, or hidden. Lighting and Camera work together but remain separate: Lighting determines the light; Camera determines the observation. Atmosphere is the medium through which both operate.

---

## 10. VISIBILITY AND CAMERA SEPARATION

### 10.1 PRINCIPLE

Camera determines what may be observed.
Lighting determines how clearly it may be observed.

These responsibilities must remain separate.

### 10.2 BOUNDARY

- The Camera department (Book III) determines: field of view, occlusion, line of sight, observer position, what is within the frame.
- The Lighting department (Book VI) determines: illumination level, shadow structure, contrast, what is lit, what is dark, how atmosphere modifies visibility.

A subject may be within the Camera's field of view but in darkness — the Camera says it is observable, Lighting says it is not perceivable. Both are correct. The subject exists in the frame but the audience cannot see it. This is intentional withholding through light, not through camera position.

Conversely, a subject may be brilliantly lit but outside the Camera's field of view — Lighting says it is perceivable, Camera says it is not observable. The audience cannot see it because the camera is not looking at it. This is withholding through observation, not through light.

### 10.3 COLLABORATION

The Lighting department reads the camera_specification (Book III) to understand:
- What is within the frame (and therefore needs illumination consideration)
- What is outside the frame (and therefore does not need illumination, unless it casts light or shadow into the frame)
- The observer position (which determines which surfaces are visible and therefore need lighting)
- The atmospheric conditions noted by the Camera (which Lighting must support with light sources that make atmosphere visible)

Lighting does not alter the camera_specification. It works within it.

## 11. EMOTIONAL SUPPORT

### 11.1 PRINCIPLE

Lighting never invents emotion. Lighting amplifies existing emotion.

### 11.2 THE PIPELINE

```
Director defines emotional intent (director_report)
  ↓
Cinematic Language translates to visual behaviour (cinematic_language_specification)
  ↓
Camera determines observation (camera_specification)
  ↓
Lighting determines illumination (lighting_specification)
  ↓
(Book VIII: Sound, Book IX: Editor, Book XIII: Prompt Compiler)
```

### 11.3 EMOTIONAL AMPLIFICATION TABLE

| Director's Emotional Intent | Lighting Behaviour | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Safety | Even illumination, low contrast, warm key, gentle fill. No deep shadows. | The audience perceives the space as open and knowable. |
| Danger | Uneven illumination, high contrast, hard shadows, deep dark areas. Sources feel unstable or insufficient. | The audience perceives the space as threatening and unknowable. |
| Loss | Reduced illumination, shadows encroaching on previously lit areas, warm light fading to cool. | The audience perceives the withdrawal of comfort. |
| Discovery | A new light source reveals previously hidden space. Shadows retreat. | The audience perceives revelation. |
| Hope | Light increases or warms. Direction of light leads toward open space. | The audience perceives possibility. |
| Isolation | Single, harsh source. Deep shadows surrounding the subject. No fill. | The audience perceives the subject's exposure and loneliness. |
| Memory | Soft, diffused, slightly sourceless light. Gentle contrast. No hard edges. | The audience perceives the scene as recalled rather than present. |
| Suspicion | Normal illumination with shadows in unexpected places. A light source that should not be there. A shadow that implies something unseen. | The audience perceives wrongness without identifying it. |

### 11.4 RULE

The emotional amplification must match the Director's emotional arc (Book V). If the Director specifies a beginning emotion of "quiet hope" and an ending emotion of "dread," the lighting must shift from the Hope behaviour to the Danger behaviour across the scene. The shift is caused by something in the scene — not by arbitrary lighting change.

## 12. LIGHT QUALITY

### 12.1 HARD VS SOFT

| Quality | Behaviour | Narrative Use |
|---|---|---|
| Hard | Sharp shadows, high contrast, directional. Sources are small or far. | Authority, exposure, interrogation, harshness, clarity that cannot be escaped. |
| Soft | Diffused shadows, low contrast, omnidirectional scatter. Sources are large or diffused. | Comfort, intimacy, memory, gentleness, ambiguity. |
| Mixed | Hard key with soft fill. | Balanced realism. The default for most scenes. |
| Specular | Small, bright reflections on surfaces. | Wetness, metal, eyes — surfaces that catch and redirect light. Draws attention. |

### 12.2 RULE

Light quality is chosen to support the emotional amplification. Hard light for exposure and authority. Soft light for comfort and memory. The choice must be justified by the Director's intent, not by aesthetic preference.

---

## 13. LIGHT DIRECTION

### 13.1 PRINCIPLE

Where light comes from determines what the audience sees first.

### 13.2 DIRECTIONAL MEANING

| Direction | Effect |
|---|---|
| From above | Authority, judgment, exposure. The subject is looked down upon by the light. |
| From below | Unnatural, menacing, distortion. Shadows invert. The subject feels wrong. |
| From the side | Division. Half-lit, half-shadowed. Internal conflict, duality, hidden self. |
| From behind (backlight) | Separation from background. Halo effect. The subject is distinguished but their face is obscured. Mystery, reverence. |
| From behind subject (front-lit from camera perspective) | Full visibility. The audience sees everything. Flat, unflattering, exposing. |
| From within | Source originates near or on the subject. Intimacy, interiority, self-knowledge. |

### 13.3 RULE

Light direction is chosen to support the Cinematic Language department's visual grammar. If the visual grammar calls for Authority, light comes from above. If it calls for Suspicion, light comes from an unexpected direction. If it calls for Isolation, light is directional with no fill.

---

## 14. CONTRAST

### 14.1 PRINCIPLE

Contrast is the ratio between the brightest and darkest parts of the observed scene.

### 14.2 CONTRAST LEVELS

| Level | Ratio | Narrative Use |
|---|---|---|
| Low contrast | Small difference between light and dark. | Comfort, safety, memory, softness. Nothing is withheld. |
| Medium contrast | Moderate difference. | Normal, balanced, realistic. The default. |
| High contrast | Large difference. | Tension, drama, conflict. Extremes of visibility and darkness. |
| Extreme contrast | Near-binary, light vs dark. | Noir, obsession, moral absolutism, inner conflict externalised. |

### 14.3 RULE

Contrast level is chosen to support the emotional arc. A scene transitioning from safety to danger increases contrast. A scene transitioning from conflict to calm decreases contrast.

## 15. COLOR OF LIGHT

### 15.1 SCOPE

Lighting owns **illumination color** — the color of the light sources themselves.

Color grading, LUTs, film stock emulation, and renderer color science do NOT belong here. Those are post-production concerns for future books.

### 15.2 LIGHT COLOR TEMPERATURE

| Source Type | Color Character | Narrative Association |
|---|---|---|
| Daylight (sun, sky) | Neutral to cool white | Reality, presence, objectivity. |
| Warm tungsten / fire | Orange-amber | Comfort, intimacy, home, danger (fire). |
| Cool moonlight / overcast | Blue-white | Coldness, isolation, otherworldliness, melancholy. |
| Fluorescent / artificial | Greenish or harsh white | Institutional, uncomfortable, unnatural, unease. |
| Neon / colored | Saturated color | Urban, artificial, stylized, mood-specific. |
| Mixed color sources | Competing colors | Conflict, duality, tension between spaces. |

### 15.3 RULE

Light color is chosen to support the emotional amplification. Warm light for comfort and home. Cool light for isolation and melancholy. Mixed colors for conflict. The color must be justified by the light source — a warm glow implies fire or tungsten, a cool wash implies moonlight or overcast sky.

### 15.4 BOUNDARY

Lighting does not determine the final color of the image. Lighting determines the color of the light in the scene. How that light is captured, processed, and graded belongs to future departments and post-production concerns.

## 16. PHOTOMETRY AND COLOR SCIENCE

### 16.1 COLOR TEMPERATURE (KELVIN)

Every light source has a correlated color temperature (CCT) measured in Kelvin (K). CCT determines the perceived warmth or coolness of the light.

| Source | Kelvin (K) | Color Character | Narrative Association |
|---|---|---|---|
| Candle/flame | 1500-1900 | Deep orange-red | Intimacy, danger (fire), primal |
| Tungsten household | 2700-3000 | Warm amber | Home, comfort, nostalgia |
| Tungsten studio | 3200 | Warm white | Controlled interior lighting |
| Sunrise/sunset | 3500-4500 | Golden warm | Magic hour, romance, beauty |
| Fluorescent | 4000-5000 | Greenish-white | Institutional, uncomfortable |
| Daylight (noon) | 5500-5600 | Neutral white | Reality, presence, objectivity |
| Overcast sky | 6000-7500 | Cool white | Melancholy, cold, sadness |
| Shade | 7000-8000 | Blue-white | Isolation, mystery, cold |
| Blue sky | 10000-12000 | Deep blue | Otherworldly, night, extreme cold |

**Rule:** All light sources in a scene should match CCT unless creative contrast is intended. Mixed CCT (e.g., 3200K tungsten with 5600K daylight) creates visual tension and should be motivated.

### 16.2 LIGHT MEASUREMENT

Illuminance (how much light falls on a surface) is measured in lux (lumens/m²) or foot-candles.

| Term | Unit | Definition | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Luminous flux | lumen (lm) | Total light output | Fixture specification |
| Illuminance | lux (lx) | Light per unit area at subject | Exposure planning |
| Luminous intensity | candela (cd) | Light in a specific direction | Beam focus |
| Luminance | candela/m² (nit) | Light reflected from surface | What the camera sees |

### 16.3 INVERSE-SQUARE LAW

Illuminance decreases proportionally to the square of distance from the source:

```
E₂ = E₁ × (d₁/d₂)²
```

Where E = illuminance, d = distance.

**Practical rules:**
- Doubling distance → light falls to 1/4 (approx -2 stops)
- Halving distance → light increases 4x (approx +2 stops)
- Moving light from 1m to 2m = -2 stops
- Moving light from 1m to 4m = -4 stops

**Example:** If a fixture produces 2500 lux at 1m, and you place it at 2.5m:
E = 2500 × (1/2.5)² = 2500 × 0.16 = 400 lux at subject

### 16.4 LIGHTING RATIOS

The lighting ratio (key light to fill light) determines contrast and mood.

| Ratio | Key:Fill | Shadow Detail | Narrative Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1:1 | Equal | No shadows — flat | Commercial, beauty, corporate |
| 2:1 | Key is 2x brighter | Soft shadows | Natural, flattering, interview |
| 3:1 | Key is 3x brighter | Moderate shadows | Cinematic narrative, drama |
| 4:1 | Key is 4x brighter | Strong shadows | Dramatic, film noir |
| 8:1 | Key is 8x brighter | Deep/crushed shadows | Film noir, horror, thriller |
| 16:1+ | Extreme | Near-black shadows | Expressionist, stylized |

**Setting the ratio:**
- Fill at 50% of key = 2:1 ratio (soft, natural)
- Fill at 25% of key = 4:1 ratio (dramatic)
- Fill at 12.5% of key = 8:1 ratio (extreme)

### 16.5 COLOR RENDERING METRICS

| Metric | Full Name | What It Measures | Professional Standard |
|---|---|---|---|
| CRI | Color Rendering Index | Color accuracy vs reference (0-100) | Ra ≥ 90 for film |
| TM-30 Rf | Fidelity Index | Modern color accuracy (0-100) | Rf ≥ 95 for professional |
| TM-30 Rg | Gamut Index | Saturation/desaturation relative to reference | Rg near 100 |
| SSI | Spectral Similarity Index | SPD match to reference illuminant | Used by rental houses |
| TLCI | Television Lighting Consistency Index | Color rendering for camera sensors | Used for broadcast |

**Practical rule:** Use fixtures with published TM-30 values and spectral power distribution (SPD) graphs. CRI alone is insufficient — two lights with identical CRI can render skin tones differently.

### 16.6 PRACTICAL LIGHT INTEGRATION

When practical lights (visible in-frame sources) are part of the set:

1. The practical's CCT should match the lighting kit's key light, or the difference should be intentional.
2. Practicals should have dimmers or controlled bulbs for intensity matching.
3. The practical may serve as a motivated light source — a desk lamp that is the character's only light.
4. Substitute LED bulbs with good SPD in practicals when possible to avoid flicker and color shifts.

### 16.7 LIGHT POSITIONING COORDINATES

Light positions are specified relative to subject using the clock-dial + elevation system:

Horizontal plane (bird's eye view — subject at center, camera at 6 o'clock):
- 6:00 = camera position (light next to camera = flat)
- 4:00-5:00 = standard key position (¾ front)
- 3:00 = 90° side light (half face lit, half shadow)
- 9:00 = standard fill position (opposite key)
- 11:00 = standard backlight position
- 12:00 = directly behind subject (rim/hair light)

Vertical plane (elevation angles):
- 0° = eye level (flat)
- 15-30° = standard key elevation (above eye line)
- 45° = dramatic top light
- 90° = directly overhead

**Alternative: XYZ World Coordinates**
For precise specification, use world-space coordinates in meters:
```
key_light:
  position_world_xyz_m: [1.84, 3.10, 1.84]
  luminous_intensity_cd: 11745
  cct_k: 3200
  beam_angle_deg: 105.9
```

## 18. EXECUTION POSITION

Book VI formally establishes Lighting's position in the execution order.

### 18.1 EXECUTION ORDER

The full execution order is defined in Book I, Section 11 (Execution Order). The Lighting department works after Camera and before Sound.

Lighting reads:
- `camera_specification` (Book III)
- `environment_specification` (Book X)
- `spatial_staging_specification` (Book VII)
- `cinematic_language_specification` (Book IV)
- `director_report` (Book V)
- `world_state` (Book II)

Lighting produces:
- `lighting_specification`

### 18.2 WHY LIGHTING COMES AFTER CAMERA

Lighting must know what the Camera has decided is observable. Lighting illuminates what the Camera has framed. If the Camera has decided that a subject is in the frame, Lighting must determine how that subject is lit. If the Camera has decided something is outside the frame, Lighting does not need to illuminate it (unless it casts light or shadow into the frame).

Lighting also reads the camera_specification's atmospheric notes to determine how light interacts with the atmosphere the Camera has noted.

### 18.3 WHY LIGHTING COMES BEFORE SOUND, EDITOR, AND PRODUCER

Sound (Book VIII) may need to know about light-related phenomena that produce sound (a buzzing bulb, a crackling fire, the hiss of rain in light). Editor (Book IX) may need to know about lighting changes that affect cutting rhythm (a light going out is a natural cut point). Producer (Book XII) needs all documents before merging.

## 20. LIGHT CONTINUITY IN THE WORLD

### 20.1 WORLD ENGINE INTEGRATION

Lighting state is part of the World Engine (Book II). The World tracks:

- **Per-location light state**: which light sources are active in each location, their current intensity, color, and quality.
- **Natural light progression**: the time of day (from world_state) determines the natural light state — sun position, sky character, moon phase.
- **Atmospheric state**: current weather conditions (from world_state) determine atmospheric effects on light.
- **Damage to light sources**: a broken bulb, a smashed lantern, an extinguished fire — these persist in the World.
- **Lighting history**: significant lighting events (power loss, fire, sunset) are logged in the timeline.

### 20.2 CONTINUITY UPDATE

After each scene, the Lighting department's changes are written back to the World:
- New light sources introduced are added to the location's state.
- Sources extinguished or damaged are updated.
- Natural light progression is advanced if time has passed.
- Atmospheric changes are recorded.

This happens silently through the World Engine's continuity protocol (Book II, Section 6).
